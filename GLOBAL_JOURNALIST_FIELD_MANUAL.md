# Global Journalist Field Manual

**Draft blueprint v0.1** · For independent journalists, local reporters, community archivists, researchers, fact-checkers, translators, and student newsrooms.

This is a practical workflow, not legal advice, a credential, or a guarantee of safety. Local laws, source-protection rules, data-protection duties, and physical risks vary. High-risk work requires qualified local legal and digital-security support.

## Operating principles

1. **Public interest, not curiosity.** State the question and why answering it matters.
2. **Minimize harm.** Collect the least personal information needed.
3. **Source before assertion.** Map every material factual claim to a source or clearly labeled inference.
4. **Preserve context.** Record original language, dates, authority, scope, and limitations.
5. **Separate evidence classes.** Distinguish primary documents, observations, witness accounts, secondary reporting, analysis, and hypotheses.
6. **Reproducibility.** Another qualified researcher should be able to repeat key public-source checks.
7. **Correction is part of the record.** Document corrections, disputes, and withdrawals without silently rewriting history.
8. **No false certainty.** A hash proves byte consistency, not truth, authorship, authenticity, completeness, or legality.
9. **Human accountability.** AI may assist discovery or organization; a responsible human verifies every published claim.
10. **Decentralization with consent.** Nodes are independently operated, opt-in, transparent about replication, and free to leave.

## Seven-stage workflow

### 1. Define the question
Write one answerable public-interest question. Define geographic and time boundaries, affected communities, evidence that would support or weaken the hypothesis, and what is out of scope. Record competing explanations before collection.

### 2. Plan safety and legal boundaries
Complete the risk assessment template before collection. Identify risks to reporters, sources, bystanders, and subjects. Prefer public records when they can answer the question. Do not bypass access controls, impersonate people, trespass, stalk, dox, or target private individuals. Do not put confidential source identities in OJEK. If confidential intake is needed, use a specialist-reviewed secure channel such as a properly operated SecureDrop deployment; do not improvise one here. Pause if risk cannot be reduced to an acceptable level.

### 3. Discover and register sources
Prefer original, authoritative, relevant materials: laws, court opinions, public meeting records, budgets, procurement records, inspection reports, public notices, official datasets, and on-record statements. Independent reporting and expert analysis can provide context and leads. Social posts and user-generated media are leads requiring verification, not self-authenticating proof.

Register canonical URL, publisher, source type, language, publication/update date, date observed, retrieval method, rights status, and limitations. Use unknown rather than guessing.

### 4. Preserve proportionately
Default to metadata, source links, and integrity digests. Preserve full content only with a documented lawful rights basis and safety review. Keep originals separate from working copies. Record collector, time, and tool/version. Keep originals distinct from translations and annotations. Never put confidential identities, credentials, private addresses, or sensitive personal data in public repositories or append-only logs. Record redactions and reasons.

### 5. Verify and corroborate
Locate the closest available primary source. Check origin, date, context, authority, and whether the item changed. Seek corroboration that is independent rather than circular. Test alternative explanations and actively seek disconfirming evidence.

For images and video, document original URL, uploader context, earliest located instance, time/location methodology, edits or gaps, and uncertainty. Do not infer identity or intent from weak visual matches. Publish only the location precision needed for public interest.

### 6. Analyze and write
Use the investigation brief, claim-evidence matrix, and verification log templates. Label facts, attributed statements, analysis, and unresolved questions. Quantify uncertainty only where a defined method supports it. Explain the method enough for readers to repeat key checks. Give subjects a fair, proportionate opportunity to respond when appropriate and safe. Do not publish raw data dumps when a careful explanation serves the public better.

### 7. Review, publish, maintain
Complete the pre-publication review checklist. After publication, monitor credible corrections and new primary sources. Keep a visible correction log. Preserve prior versions where safe and lawful; remove or redact dangerous personal information when necessary. Make datasets and methods available only where rights and safety permit.

## Confidence language

- **Directly documented:** the cited primary record directly states or records the fact.
- **Corroborated:** multiple independent sources support the material point.
- **Supported but incomplete:** evidence supports the point, but material gaps remain.
- **Disputed:** credible sources materially conflict.
- **Unverified lead:** worth checking, not a publishable fact.
- **Not established:** available evidence does not support the claim.
- **Corrected / withdrawn:** prior publication or observation has been amended or withdrawn with a dated explanation.

Avoid numerical confidence scores unless the method is defined, calibrated, and explained.

## Decentralized interoperability

OJEK is a local evidence and provenance layer, not a central authority. Independent nodes may keep local records, verify packages, publish signed manifests when safe, replicate only permitted records, quarantine unsafe material, and maintain local correction and takedown procedures. A node is not authoritative merely because it is connected or has a valid signature.

## Explicit limitations

This manual does not provide covert collection tactics, confidential-source intake, legal advice, forensic certification, automated truth scoring, or a promise of anonymity. It does not authorize surveillance, harassment, doxxing, or collection of private information. It requires review by journalists, editors, archivists, translators, security practitioners, and affected communities worldwide.
