# Global Rights and Law Atlas — Project Proposal

## Purpose
Create a multilingual, source-linked public reference collection for foundational documents, constitutional texts, statutes, human-rights instruments, international agreements, and official protections.

This is a companion data project for the Open Journalism Evidence Kit. It is an index and research tool—not a legal advice service and not an assertion that one country's law applies everywhere.

## Coverage model

### United States
Begin with primary and official sources:
- Declaration of Independence
- Articles of Confederation
- Constitution of the United States
- Bill of Rights and subsequent amendments
- Federal statutes and United States Code
- Code of Federal Regulations
- Supreme Court opinions and selected federal appellate decisions
- State constitutions, statutes, regulations, and official court materials
- Foundational state and colonial documents where public-domain status and provenance are clear

The First Amendment is a major U.S. constitutional protection for speech, press, religion, assembly, and petition. It is an important part of the U.S. collection, not a universal substitute for the distinct constitutional and legal traditions of other countries.

### International and regional instruments
Build a catalog of authoritative sources, including:
- United Nations Charter
- Universal Declaration of Human Rights
- International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights
- International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights
- Convention against Torture
- Convention on the Rights of the Child
- Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women
- Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities
- Refugee Convention and Protocol
- Geneva Conventions and relevant protocols
- International Labour Organization conventions
- UNESCO instruments relevant to education, culture, science, and freedom of expression
- Regional human-rights instruments and institutions in Africa, the Americas, Europe, Asia-Pacific, and the Middle East where authoritative texts are available

Record treaty status, ratification, reservations, declarations, entry into force, and jurisdictional applicability from authoritative treaty databases. Do not infer that a state is bound by a treaty merely because the text exists.

### National and subnational law
For each jurisdiction, prioritize:
1. Official gazette or government legislation portal
2. Official constitution or parliamentary source
3. Official court or judiciary source
4. Treaty depositary or recognized international organization
5. Reputable secondary source only as a clearly labeled aid

Each item should identify jurisdiction, issuing body, document type, official source, publication/effective dates, amendment history, language, legal status, and last verification date.

## Data model
A legal-document record should contain:
- Stable project identifier
- Official title and alternative titles
- Jurisdiction and territorial scope
- Issuing authority
- Document type
- Official source URL
- Original language(s)
- Publication, adoption, entry-into-force, and effective dates where applicable
- Current status: in force, amended, repealed, expired, superseded, unknown
- Amendment and version relationships
- Treaty ratification/reservation metadata where applicable
- Translation provenance and status
- Copyright, database-rights, and reuse terms
- Last checked timestamp
- Editorial review state

## Legal accuracy safeguards
- Preserve the original text and source link.
- Distinguish enacted text from proposals, summaries, commentary, and unofficial translations.
- Never silently replace an earlier version.
- Show amendment and repeal history.
- Display the jurisdiction and effective date prominently.
- Label unofficial translations and machine translations.
- Do not present an AI-generated summary as the law itself.
- Link to the official text for every legal proposition.
- Explain that applicability depends on jurisdiction, facts, interpretation, and current law.
- Provide a correction process and review history.

## Rights and reuse
Public availability does not automatically mean every document, translation, database, or annotation is free of copyright or reuse restrictions. Record the source's terms and jurisdiction-specific status. Prefer official public-domain or openly licensed texts and link rather than republish where rights are uncertain.

## Governance
The Atlas should be maintained as a separate repository or clearly separated dataset. Contributions require source verification, jurisdictional metadata, and review by people with relevant legal or language expertise. It must not be marketed as legal advice or as a substitute for local counsel.
